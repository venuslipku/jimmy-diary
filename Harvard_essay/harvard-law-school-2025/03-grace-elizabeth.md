# Grace Elizabeth's Essay

**Collection:** Successful Harvard Law School Essays (2025)  
**Source:** <https://business.thecrimson.com/successful-harvard-law-school-essays-2025>  
**Essay #:** 3

## Prompt

This essay responds to the following prompt: The Admissions Committee makes every effort to understand who you are as an individual and potential Harvard Law School student and graduate. Please share how your experiences, background, and/or interests have shaped you and will shape your engagement in the HLS community and the legal profession.

## Essay

“Welcome! My name is Grace, and I will be taking care of y’all this evening.” The first time I uttered those words, I had no idea the impact they would have on my life. I was 18 when I started working at my hometown’s most famous restaurant. Unbeknownst to me, working as a waitress was about to radically shift my perspective.

I will never forget the first time I walked into the kitchen. My favorite restaurant was no longer a sliver of paradise; it was as if a veil had suddenly been lifted. The thermometer read 118 degrees. Men and women were hollering at each other in a language I did not understand. Some people glared at me, others smiled, and some did not seem to take any notice of my presence at all. I felt like a deer in the headlights. I was no longer the young girl sitting on the patio at table 5. I became just another part of a well-oiled machine. Anyone who has worked in a restaurant can understand how crazy and hectic it can be. Now, imagine that environment in a language you don’t speak. How do you communicate “avocado allergy” to someone who doesn’t speak the same language?

I quickly adapted, picking up some Spanish phrases here and there and eventually making friends with many of the Spanish-speaking people in the kitchen. Waitressing was difficult work, but I soon began to love it. I have worked there seasonally for three years now. I am not the same person today that I was when I started, primarily because of an event that took place my first summer.

The sun was inescapable, the wind was nonexistent, and the humidity was stifling— it was uncharacteristically hot, even for late July in Texas. I was working on the outdoor patio, and when I wasn’t frantically re-filling customers’ glasses with water, I was in the bussing station chugging my water bottle and trying to avoid the scorching Texas heat that was draining energy from me minute by minute. I was working alongside a busboy named Carlos, who could not have been more than 16 or 17. He was very thin, jumpy, and

did not speak any English. As I walked into the kitchen to check on the status of my table’s fajitas, I heard a huge commotion. Whether it was from heat exhaustion, dehydration, or a combination of the two, Carlos had collapsed. I heard someone yell out, “Call an ambulance!” The managers rushed in to assess the situation and call for help when suddenly, a dishwasher - who I would later recognize as Carlos’s uncle - burst into the kitchen. “No!”, he exclaimed, “No llames a los paramédicos”. The managers tried changing his mind, but the man was unrelenting.

The realization hit me as hard as the Texas sun beat down: Carlos couldn’t be helped. He was undocumented, and calling for help could put him at risk of deportation. It was later shared with me that the young boy fled from El Salvador to the United States that very summer, escaping life in gangs. While I was there making some pocket money, young Carlos was there to survive. I realized that there was an entire class of people - undocumented immigrants - living in the shadows of American society, people working to make a better living for themselves, out of necessity and not for pocket change. It was humbling to realize my lack of understanding.

Waitressing has taught me valuable skills: multitasking, effective communication, time management, and customer service, all while honing my ability to remain calm and composed in a fast-paced environment. But when I reflect on my time as a waitress, those skills are not what come to mind. Instead, I think about the people I have worked with. I think about the dishwasher who could not afford to take a few days off work following the death of his sick mother. I think about the bartender who works two shifts a day,
seven days a week, 52 weeks a year, in order to send money back to his family in Mexico. I think about the busboy who had to repeat his freshman year of high school because he did not have enough time to complete his homework between shifts. I think about the bar-back who spends every holiday by himself because he has no family in the States. Finally, I think about Carlos.

My relationships with these people have made me aware of the privileges that I took for granted every day, namely, being born on American soil. My experience as a waitress has made me realize that I want to go to law school to learn how I might do whatever possible to help people: whether it be immigrants like Carlos or anyone in need. I believe that the legal profession provides a powerful platform for advocating for justice and equality, and I am committed to using my education and skills to make a meaningful difference in the lives of those who are often marginalized and underserved. Through law school, I intend to not only gain the knowledge necessary for this endeavor but also to continue building the empathy and understanding that have been fostered by my time as a waitress.

## Professional Review by LSATMax

Details, details, details.

If we could sum-up Grace Elizabeth’s essay in just three words, that would be it. This story is vivid. The reader can sense the heat of the kitchen, hear the yells of restaurant workers, and feel the sense of urgency among the characters. This is a great example of “show, don’t tell” when writing.

But this wasn’t just a random hook to catch the readers’ attention, either, this story led to real character growth and motivation to become a lawyer. Grace Elizabeth was accepted to Harvard Law because her personal narrative demonstrates a rare combination of emotional intelligence, humility, and moral clarity—qualities essential for the study and practice of law. Rather than relying on flashy achievements or name-dropping credentials, she uses a deeply human story to illustrate her growing awareness of systemic inequality, particularly regarding undocumented immigrants. Her ability to recognize privilege, empathize across language and cultural barriers, and respond with a desire to act, not just reflect, sets her apart.

The icing on the cake is that all of this connects to her interest in the law. This is what makes it a strong law school personal statement—connecting a personal story to the motivation to study law. This “formula” gives the complete picture of you as an applicant.

In the end, this essay succeeds because the author frames her experiences not as obstacles or resume-builders, but as opportunities to learn, to listen, and to grow. Admissions committees look for applicants who not only recognize such moments but also seize them and reflect meaningfully on their significance. The essay captures that perfectly.
